How I built TallyPay

“I've paid.” “I haven't got it.” Both people can be telling the truth. Between the customer and the shop sits a mobile-money provider whose answer can get lost, come back three times or never come. It can even turn a payment down and take the money anyway.

I wanted to see what it takes to keep honest books in the middle of that. TallyPay is my answer. It's a portfolio project, so the provider and the money are made up. I tried to make the trouble realistic.

What I chose

  • The database does the accounting

    Each movement of money is an entry whose lines add up to zero. Postgres checks the sum at the end, and after that nobody can change or delete the entry. I didn't want the books to depend on my code being right.

  • Note it down, then call

    Before it calls the provider, TallyPay saves when it will ask again. The payment's own number goes with the call, so asking twice never charges twice. If the app dies halfway, the work is still waiting.

  • One request, one payment

    The idempotency key, the payment and the answer are saved together. Send the same request twice at the same moment and you get one payment, and the same answer both times.

  • The statement settles it

    Once a day, the books are compared with the provider's statement. Money nobody expected goes into an account of its own until someone explains it. One person suggests what to do, and somebody else has to agree.

  • A queue made of rows

    Work to do sits in a Postgres table, saved with whatever asked for it. QStash only wakes the app at the right time. A daily call picks up what it missed.

  • Plain pages

    The server sends HTML, with a little HTMX. Every form works without JavaScript, and only the site's own scripts can run.

  • A demo that keeps everything

    Each visitor gets a new shop. A day later its users, keys and addresses are closed, but the entries stay, because deleting them would break the one rule I care about. A demo takes about 33 KB, and only so many can be open at once.

How I tested it

  • On a real Postgres, with many threads doing the same thing at once.
  • With random failures, crashes and repeated calls, some over 60 days. In the end, every payment must have been charged once and the books must match the statement.
  • By breaking the protections one by one, to check that a test fails each time.

What it can't do

  • The provider lives inside the same app. A real one would need its own connection and its own certification.
  • Users are created with a command. No invitations, no password reset, no second factor.
  • Idempotency keys never expire.
  • Reconciliation reads the whole statement. Fine for a demo, not for a million payments.
  • A demo's sample payments have past dates, but the books date them from the day it was opened.
  • It runs on free plans. Past their daily limit, webhooks and payments wait for the daily call.
